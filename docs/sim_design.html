The description, as the paper would give it

Counts were simulated from the log1p NMF model itself, with parameters calibrated so that the simulated data reproduce the count characteristics of the pancreas dataset. For each of ten seeds we drew an \(n \times K\) loading matrix \(L\) with rows \(\mathbf{l}_{i\cdot} \sim \textrm{Dirichlet}(0.3, \dots, 0.3)\), so that every sample concentrates on a small number of factors, and a \(p \times K\) factor matrix \(F\) with independent entries that are zero with probability \(\pi_0\) and otherwise \(\exp(\mu + \sigma t)\), \(t \sim t_4\), truncated to \([T_{\textrm{lo}}, T]\) — a point mass at zero and a heavy-tailed body, truncated on both sides. Each feature was conditioned to load on at least one factor, and the lower truncation guarantees it does so at a detectable level; together these are the analogue of filtering genes for detectable expression. Counts were then drawn as \(y_{ij} \sim \textrm{Poisson}(\lambda_{ij})\) with \(\lambda_{ij} = e^{\theta_{ij}} - 1\) and \(\Theta = LF^{\top}\), i.e. from the model at \(c = 1\). The four constants \((\pi_0, \mu, \sigma, T)\) were chosen by matching two properties of the real data: the mean count, matched exactly, and the curve \(c \mapsto \operatorname{pm}(\log(1 + Y/c))\) — the peak-to-mean ratio of the data on the link scale, which by [the sparsity results] governs the factor sparsity attainable at each \(c\) — matched in least squares across \(c \in [10^{-4}, 10^{4}]\), after adjusting the real curve to the simulation’s size. The resulting datasets are 88–89% zeros with a mean count of about 0.6 and maximum counts in the low thousands, against 82%, 0.67, and (size-adjusted) $$8,800 for the real data.

Everything in that paragraph is verified below on the ten seeds the experiment will actually use.

The generative model, precisely

For seed \(s \in \{1, \dots, 10\}\):

  1. \(\mathbf{l}_{i\cdot} \sim \textrm{Dirichlet}(a, \dots, a)\) independently, \(a = 0.3\), giving \(L \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{500 \times 5}\).
  2. \(u_{jk} \sim \textrm{Unif}(0,1)\) and \(t_{jk} \sim t_{4}\) independently, and \[f_{jk} = \textrm{clamp}\left(\exp(\mu + \sigma t_{jk});\; T_{\textrm{lo}},\, T\right) \cdot \mathbf{1}\{u_{jk} > \pi_0\},\] except that a feature \(j\) with \(u_{jk} \leq \pi_0\) for every \(k\) keeps its single largest-\(u_{jk}\) factor (so no feature is dead; deterministic given \(u\)).
  3. \(\Theta = LF^{\top}\), \(\;\lambda_{ij} = c_{\textrm{true}}(e^{\theta_{ij}} - 1)\) with \(c_{\textrm{true}} = 1\), \(\;y_{ij} \sim \textrm{Poisson}(\lambda_{ij})\). In the rare event a row or column of \(Y\) is entirely zero, the Poisson draw (only) is repeated with the next sub-seed; the fitting code rejects all-zero columns, as do real pipelines.

There are no size factors (\(s_i = 1\); everything is fit with s = FALSE). The two truncation points have distinct jobs. The cap bounds the rates at \(e^{T} - 1 \approx 2341\), which is what makes the maximum count land in the same place on every seed — unbounded heavy tails made \(\max(Y)\) swing by an order of magnitude across seeds when we tried them. The floor \(T_{\textrm{lo}} = 0.05\) exists because a feature active in a single factor can otherwise draw a magnitude so small that its column of \(Y\) is all zero with probability near one, which the fitting code rejects — the seed-7 dataset did exactly this before the floor was added. At the floor, a single-factor feature’s column has expected total count \(\approx T_{\textrm{lo}} \cdot n/K = 5\). The floor moves about 4% of the nonzero entries; the knobs above were recalibrated with it in place.

How the four calibrated numbers were chosen

The target is the real pancreas count matrix (7,606 cells \(\times\) 18,195 genes; 82.2% zeros, mean 0.674). Two properties are matched:

  • The budget curve \(c \mapsto \operatorname{pm}(\log(1+Y/c))\), in least squares on the log scale over the experiment’s own \(c\) grid. This curve depends only on the count histogram, and it is the quantity our sparsity theory identifies as governing what any fit can do at each \(c\). Because it involves a maximum it must be size-adjusted: the target uses \(t^{*} = 8820\), the median maximum of \(np = 250000\) draws from the real marginal, not the real matrix’s own maximum (19487).
  • The mean count, exactly. The approximation experiments consume the absolute scale of the counts, and the curve alone pins it only loosely. So \(\mu\) is not searched: inside every calibration step it is solved by one-dimensional root-finding so the expected mean count equals the real 0.674 (profiling). Nelder–Mead searches only \((\pi_0, \sigma, T)\) against the curve, with common random numbers across evaluations; the final \(\mu\) is re-solved on 20 seeds.

The calibration code is calibrate_approx_sim.R, next to the experiment scripts; it writes approx_sim_calibration.rds, from which the knobs in the table above were checked.

Honest limits of the match at K = 5

The same calibration machinery matched the pancreas curve to within \(\pm 16\%\) at \(600 \times 600\), \(K = 10\) (git history of this page). At \(K = 5\) it cannot do as well, and the reason is structural: with five factors and Dirichlet-peaked rows, each entry’s rate is nearly a single-factor draw, and the knobs cannot feed the zero fraction, the mean, and the extreme tail simultaneously. Two richer variants — a free tail degree of freedom, and two-component magnitudes — fixed one end of the curve only by sacrificing the other, and were rejected. The realized compromise, visible in the tables above: zeros ~0.89 vs 0.822 (so the budget curve runs ~1.4–1.5\(\times\) high at the small-\(c\) end), and a maximum around 2,000 vs the size-adjusted 8,820 (so the curve runs ~0.3–0.8\(\times\) at the large-\(c\) end). The mean is centered by construction. If the tail mismatch ever matters, the honest fix is \(K = 10\), not more knobs.
